An alum has launched EdgeTech, a start-up that makes smart insoles for hockey skates. The insoles track pressure points and stride patterns, sending real-time feedback to an app so players can improve skating technique. Early adopters—mostly competitive youth players—love the product and share videos of their progress. Coaches also see potential for training drills. Parents raise questions about value and privacy, rather than affordability. At $500 per pair, the insoles cost about the same as two private skating lessons and far less than high-end skates (often $1,000+). The founder wonders who the real customer is: the player, the parent who pays, or the coach who influences purchase decisions. EdgeTech has sold about 200 units through word-of-mouth and local tournaments. Manufacturing is outsourced to a small electronics supplier, and each batch requires upfront payment, making inventory costly. Hardware changes are slow and expensive—each design tweak means new molds and testing. The founder has considered a “fail fast” approach but worries that discarding prototypes could burn cash quickly. Parents say they want proof that the insoles actually improve performance, not just generate cool graphs. Coaches want easy setup and reliable data during practice. Some parents suggest they might pay more if the product came with personalized drills or evidence that it reduces injuries. The founder is exploring a hardware + subscription model: for example, lowering the price of the insoles to $300 but adding $15/month for premium app features like video analysis, progress tracking, and custom drills.
### Question
Apply the lessons discussed during the “problem-first vs. solution-first” session to propose the founder’s next two validation steps. Explain why “fail fast” is tricky with hardware and suggest two approaches that the founder could take instead.
### Answer
Without mentioning his product, the founder should talk to the parents, coaches and players to figure out what problem he is trying to solve. He should ask questions like "How are skating lessons working for you?" and "What are the hardest challenges when trying to improve skating?". Based on the answers the customers give, the founder can I identify which customer his product is a fit for and how he should focus his product to make it most appealing to them. The founder can then conduct a study to show how the skates improve performance

Fail fast it tricky with hardware because there is a high cost associated with failed prototypes and it take a long time to iterate on each cycle because of manufacturing. A better approach would be to  gauge interest in the product or feature by creating a landing page or sending out surveys and deciding which features to pursue based on signups or responses. 

Another approach would be to focus on software prototyping first and do the iteration and learning on the app and its features which has a lower cost and turn around time. Test the value proposition of the subscription and the usability/demand for new features without expensive hardware changes. If users ignore a "personalized drill" feature, EdgeTech hasn't wasted money building a new sensor to enable it.
